<h2>The MVP is not the cheap version</h2>
<p>Founders usually get into trouble when they treat the MVP like a cut-price build. The real job of an MVP is sharper than that. It should prove that the market cares about the problem you are solving and that users will actually move through the core flow you designed.</p>
<p>That means the right MVP is smaller, but it should still feel deliberate. The positioning, the onboarding path, the value proposition, and the one feature that matters most all need to feel thought through.</p>
<h2>What a strong MVP actually does</h2>
<ul>
  <li>Validates the one use case people will pay attention to.</li>
  <li>Shows you where users get confused before the full roadmap exists.</li>
  <li>Creates real usage data instead of opinion-driven planning.</li>
  <li>Keeps the team focused on momentum instead of feature sprawl.</li>
</ul>
<h2>How we approach it</h2>
<p>We usually shape MVP work as a narrow execution lane: define the market angle, lock the first conversion flow, design a premium interface around that flow, then build only the systems needed for launch and learning.</p>
<p>That is what gives an MVP leverage. You move from abstract planning to measurable behaviour fast, without turning version one into a mini enterprise platform.</p>
